Writing for Answer Campus

Daniele Montefiori's Dev Log for AC 1 & 2; Log 1

Next Log →

As part of the writing team for Answer Campus for over a year and a half, one of the most significant considerations during the various stages of design for each new (or revised) narrative beat is how such beats are implemented into the existing dialogue system. During the initial development of Answer Campus (now Answer Campus: The First Semester), the writing-to-dialogue pipeline was straightforward:

  • The writing team would consult with the lead developers and the project head(s) about potential conflicts, anecdotal experiences, and community outreach to ideate on potential topics and scenes to be explored in game. These concepts were usually mapped onto specific characters, and in some cases, a character's behavior directly inspired a potential story beat.
  • Concepts that were explored in depth and received favorably could be implemented into the current narrative of the game; if so, they were delineated among the various writers based on which character(s) each writer was currently involved with (for me, this was often Breanna and Brad).
  • Each writer would then take time to map out a rough draft for their character's scene, which involved writing every possible piece of dialogue, every possible choice (with corresponding outcomes), and every possible ending to a conversation. Optionally, writers could include potential future variables or ramifications for certain choices or endings as reference.
  • Once a rough draft was completed, it was implemented into the existing dialogue system with concise edits, and subject to feedback before being revised.

For most of Answer Campus: The First Semester’s initial development after the first demo release and narrative restructuring, the final two steps of this process were slightly cumbersome. Most of the developers and the writing team (myself included) utilized Google Docs and Sheets to manage conversations and map out the narrative of The First Semester. This, of course, meant that we would not be able to see how these would play in-game until a script was drafted and then implemented into the conversation framework in Unity (which is the engine Answer Campus uses).

Answer Campus uses a custom built conversation system, primarily based around individual nodes. Initially the writing team would finalize scripts and publish them onto the Answer Campus shared drive to be implemented by the project leads and the coding team, but it was later decided that the writers themselves should be able to directly implement their scripts into the dialogue codebase and export their scenes. While this helped expedite the writing-to-dialogue process, now that the scenes didn't have to be passed over to the project lead to be implemented, it also meant that the writing team had to familiarize themselves with the codebase itself and, importantly, meticulously map the dialogue into the dialogue system (without overflowing the text box) as well as ensuring the conversation doesn’t loop or softlock at any point. This takes time and, again, you can’t see how your conversation will play out in game until you implement it. It also means you had to open Unity yourself whenever you wanted to edit dialogue; if you needed to proofread or test someone else's scene, you had to install Unity (if you didn't have it) and import their scene.

During my time writing for Answer Campus, I found myself shifting from the scriptwriting approach in favor of working directly within the dialogue codebase. I would plan and write my dialogue inside the code while working off of a general idea of how things would go, letting the dialogue itself flow naturally. This was great at expediting the implementation, since I was implementing the dialogue live, but it also meant that I would have to concern myself with the pacing and the layout of the conversation as it was being written while dealing with the intricacies of the codebase and with proofreading. This often resulted in burnout, especially when dealing with some of the bigger conversations planned.

With development starting on Answer Campus: The Second Semester, there has been discussion about revising the various pipelines for the game’s development and among them is a streamlining of the writing process and the dialogue system. Specifically, Answer Campus 2’s writing will largely be done using a newly developed export system for the game’s dialogue that can be managed and edited via Google Sheets.

With the new workflow, a writer can create and map out a scene in Unity and then, once it has been mapped out, export it to Sheets where it can be easily proofread and altered by another writer or editor without them also having to open Unity. Going forward, the hope is that this new workflow will expedite the writing process even further and centralize it, as any and all new conversations can be imported inside the existing dialogues without having to be individually uploaded and implemented.
